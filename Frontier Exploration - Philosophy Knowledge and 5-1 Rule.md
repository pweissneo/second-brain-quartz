# Frontier Exploration: Philosophy Knowledge and 5:1 Rule

## The Gap

The 5:1 personal-to-general knowledge ratio rule has edge cases for:
- Legal knowledge bases
- Creative skill domains (music composition, visual art, creative writing)

But philosophy (and similar analytical fields like history, literary criticism, cultural studies) are NOT covered. These fields have a unique characteristic:

**Philosophy requires extensive general knowledge as prerequisite for personal insights.**

## Why This Matters

In philosophy:
- General knowledge = facts about philosophical positions, arguments, historical context, what philosophers believed
- Personal knowledge = your own interpretations, original insights, critiques, applications

The problem: To have a meaningful personal philosophical insight, you MUST first understand the existing philosophical framework. A student can't critique utilitarianism without first understanding what utilitarianism is.

This differs from:
- Legal (where jurisdiction-specific implementation tips count as personal)
- Creative skills (where foundational study enables future creation)

Philosophy is about understanding and analyzing existing ideas - the "general" knowledge IS the foundation for "personal" insight.

## The Edge Case

For philosophy and similar analytical domains:

- **Foundation-first learning:** Allow higher general ratio (4:1 or 3:1) during initial study phases - understanding what philosophers believed is ESSENTIAL before you can have original thoughts
- **Analysis as personal knowledge:** Philosophical analysis, critique, comparison, and application of concepts to new contexts count as personal even when grounded in general knowledge
- **Original contribution threshold:** The test should be: "Does this note contain YOUR analysis, or just restate what others have said?" - not whether general facts are present

## Modified Test for Philosophy

For philosophy notes:
1. Does this include YOUR interpretation or critique (not just restatement)?
2. Does this apply philosophical concepts to a new context or problem you care about?
3. Does this synthesize multiple perspectives with YOUR judgment?
4. Is this documenting a philosophical position you're developing, not just describing one?

Count as personal if yes to any. Exclude purely factual notes about what philosophers believed (these are the foundation, not the personal insight).
