Seed Stress Test: 2+ Links Rule in Genealogy

Tested Rule: Every note must link to at least 2 other notes OR be tagged with foundational: true OR be tagged with specialized: true.

Test Domain: Genealogy / Family History Knowledge Base

Does the Rule Make Sense for Genealogy?

Partially. Genealogy is fundamentally about connections between people, so link density is conceptually aligned. However, natural family structures create many nodes that legitimately have fewer than 2 natural outgoing connections, especially for:

  • Distant ancestors (very deep in the tree)
  • Individuals with no recorded descendants
  • Single individuals with no family connections in the vault

The rule’s intent (preventing orphaned, invisible notes) is valid, but the implementation conflicts with how genealogical knowledge naturally structures.

Is the Test Executable?

Yes — Counting outgoing links is trivial. But the test reveals that “link count” doesn’t capture the actual connectivity in genealogy vaults. A person with only 1 child may still be well-connected through backlinks (descendants linking to them), but the rule only measures outgoing links.

Edge Cases Discovered

1. Deep Ancestors (The “Root” Problem)

Problem: In a family tree going back 10+ generations, early ancestors might only connect to:

  • Their spouse (if recorded)
  • One of their children (the ancestor in the direct line)

Example: “John Smith (1650-1720)” — your 7x great-grandfather

  • Links to: John Smith’s Spouse, Your 6x Great Father
  • Only 2 links, which passes — but barely

Analysis: This is actually a deeper problem: the 3-hop rule also conflicts with genealogy’s natural depth. But for the 2-link rule specifically, deep ancestors are at risk because:

  • They may have no recorded spouse
  • They may only have 1 child who continues the direct line
  • Any “extra” links would be decorative (linking to unrelated siblings just to meet the quota)

Recommendation: Use foundational: true for direct-line ancestors beyond 5 generations. These are structural anchors, not discoverable content.

2. Terminal Nodes (No Descendants in Vault)

Problem: Notes about family members who have no recorded descendants in the vault:

  • “Mary Johnson (1845-1910)” — had no children
  • Might only link to: Her Parents, Her Siblings
  • Only 2 links — but one is a “negative” link (she had no children)

Analysis: Terminal nodes are different from deep ancestors. Terminal nodes have clear family connections (parents, siblings) but no forward connections because there are no descendants to record.

Recommendation: Terminal individuals should link to:

  1. Parents (family of origin)
  2. Siblings (family of origin)
  3. Spouse (if any)

That’s typically 2-3 links naturally. The test should work if we ensure spouses are recorded.

3. Spouses Without Children

Problem: Notes about spouses who married into the family but have no children in the vault:

  • “Sarah Williams (1860-1940)” — married into family, no children recorded
  • Links to: Her Husband, Her Parents-in-Law
  • Only 2 links, both “through” someone else

Analysis: Spouses are structurally important but have narrow connections. They connect to their spouse and to their birth family, but the genealogical vault may not contain their birth family.

Recommendation: Allow spouses to have 1 link (to their spouse) if the spouse note contains rich information about the family they married into. Alternatively, use specialized: true for spouse notes.

4. Unconnected Siblings

Problem: When a sibling dies young or has no recorded descendants, they may only connect to:

  • Their parents
  • One sibling (if particularly close)

Analysis: This is a real gap in genealogical records, not a rule violation. The note represents real knowledge (someone existed) even if connections are limited.

Recommendation: For notes with limited connections due to incomplete records, use confidence: low and note “limited biographical information” rather than forcing decorative links.

5. Location-Based vs. Person-Based Structure

Problem: Genealogy vaults can be structured two ways:

  1. Person-based: Each note is a person (current approach)
  2. Location-based: Each note is a place, with people linked

Person-based structures create more 2-link failures. Location-based structures would have different challenges.

Analysis: The rule assumes person-based structure. For location-based genealogy vaults, different thresholds apply.

Recommendation: The Seed should clarify that the 2-link rule applies to primary entity notes (persons). Location notes, timeline notes, and source notes may have different thresholds.

6. Source Notes (The Census Problem)

Problem: Source notes like “1880 US Census” might contain 100+ names, but what do they link to?

  • Links to: Census Records, 1880 Federal Census
  • Only 2 links — but the note is rich (contains all enumerated individuals)

Analysis: Source notes are fundamentally different from person notes. They aggregate information rather than represent a single entity.

Recommendation: Source notes should use knowledge-type: source and have relaxed 2-link requirements. A census note might link to the collection and to a finding aid — that’s sufficient.

7. Timeline Notes (Era-Based Organization)

Problem: Notes organized by time period (“Smith Family 1800-1850”) might link to:

  • 1700-1800 Period (previous)
  • 1850-1900 Period (next)
  • Only 2 links

Analysis: Timeline notes are sequential by nature. They connect to neighbors in time, which is meaningful connectivity.

Recommendation: Timeline/note-based notes should pass with 2 links if they genuinely represent temporal sequence. The links are meaningful (before/after), not decorative.

8. “Orphan” Ancestors Who Are Actually Foundational

Problem: The earliest recorded ancestor in a vault might have only:

  • A spouse
  • One child (who continues the line)

That’s 2 links — passes — but this ancestor is actually foundational to the entire vault.

Analysis: The rule finds violations for nodes that are structurally critical. “Foundational” in genealogy means “without this person, this branch doesn’t exist.”

Recommendation: Add genealogical context to the foundational: true tag:

9. Family Group Notes (Hub Notes)

Problem: A note like “Smith Family of Virginia” might link to:

  • Individual family member notes (5-10)
  • Location note
  • Timeline note

That’s 3+ links naturally, passing the test.

Analysis: Hub notes in genealogy work well. They naturally aggregate related individuals.

Recommendation: No change needed — the rule works for hub notes.

10. Marriage Records Without Children

Problem: Notes about a marriage (“John Smith + Mary Jones, 1850”) might only link to:

  • John Smith
  • Mary Jones
  • Only 2 links

Analysis: Marriage records are important but structurally simple — two individuals joined.

Recommendation: Marriage notes should link to both spouses AND to their parents (connecting to families). That’s 4 links naturally if parents are in the vault.

Refinements Proposed

Refinement 1: Genealogy-Specific Foundational Tag

Refinement 2: Source Note Exception

Edge case: Source notes (census, birth records, immigration records) may have fewer than 2 outgoing links because their purpose is aggregation, not entity representation. Use knowledge-type: source and require only 1 link to the collection/finding aid.

Refinement 3: Timeline Note Exception

Edge case: Notes organized by temporal periods (eras, decades, centuries) may have exactly 2 links (previous period, next period) that represent meaningful temporal sequence. These are not decorative — they enable chronological traversal.

Refinement 4: Record Completeness Tag

This helps distinguish “limited because of vault structure” from “limited because records don’t exist.”

Does the Test Fail?

No — With appropriate edge cases, the test is executable. But the test alone isn’t enough — genealogy requires:

  1. foundational: true for direct-line ancestors
  2. specialized: true for spouses
  3. record-completeness to distinguish structure gaps from record gaps
  4. Source/timeline exceptions

The rule makes sense for genealogy but needs domain-specific adaptations.
